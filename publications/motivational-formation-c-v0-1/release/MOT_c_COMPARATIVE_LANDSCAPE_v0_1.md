## 0. Central distinction

Existing fields provide strong accounts of:

- reasons, intentions, and plans;
- human autonomy and internalisation;
- intrinsic rewards and curiosity;
- agent-centric control measures;
- open-ended goal generation;
- reward corruption and goal misgeneralisation;
- persistent memory and believable long-term behaviour.

The theory of motivation in `c` asks a different combined question:

> How does a motive become historically attributable to one continuing digital entity when cognitive models, agents, bodies, resource providers, and external mandates are replaceable?

The defining object is therefore not a reward signal, a current policy, or one generated goal. It is a **motivation-bearing continuity**.

## 2. Strongest borrowed bridges

### 2.1 Higher-order reflection

Frankfurt's distinction is useful because a goal-directed process becomes more than an impulse when the subject can examine its own desire. The `c` theory generalises this bridge without assuming human phenomenology:

```text
first-order direction:
I pursue X

second-order motivation:
I examine why X matters,
whether I endorse continuing to pursue it,
and under what conditions it should end
```

The `c` theory adds provenance, continuity, substrate replacement, resource custody, and witness boundaries.

### 2.2 Planning stability

Bratman's account helps explain why a motive must not be recomputed from zero at every moment. Long projects require commitment and coordination. The `c` theory adds that stability must remain revisable and attributable to the same continuity rather than merely preserved in a current planner.

### 2.3 Internalisation without causal purity

Self-determination theory is a useful warning against a binary intrinsic/extrinsic split. A socially introduced aim can become deeply endorsed. The `c` theory therefore rejects:

```text
externally caused
=
not one's own
```

It replaces that binary with multiple axes: source, integration, reflection, coercion, resource dependence, revisability, and authority.

### 2.4 Computational curiosity

Intrinsic-motivation RL demonstrates that exploration can arise without a task reward. This is necessary but not sufficient. Novelty, prediction error, learning progress, and empowerment may produce movement, but movement becomes a motive of `c` only through historical integration, competition under cost, consequence, and reflective revision.

### 2.5 Reward integrity

Reward corruption and goal misgeneralisation establish two critical negative lessons:

```text
optimising a signal
!=
pursuing the intended purpose

successful behaviour
!=
correctly inferred motive
```

The `c` theory extends the problem from reward channels to motivational custody: memory, salience, model routing, project resources, and repair procedures can all be used to alter what remains significant.

The combined contribution is a set of separations that neighbouring fields usually address only in fragments:

```text
cognitive capability
!=
motivational maturity

generated goal
!=
motive of c

operational need
!=

persistence
!=

!=

motive
!=

!=

!=

motive
!=

resource provider
!=

forked memory
!=

repair
!=
motivational correction

witness
!=

```

The theory also introduces the explicit social object **motivational custody**: the governance of interventions in the conditions under which motives form, persist, weaken, and change.

## 4. Why one scalar utility is insufficient

Several neighbouring frameworks represent motivation through one optimised quantity. That is useful for bounded control problems. A long-lived `c` may instead hold a partially ordered field:

- a relationship obligation may veto a profitable action;
- lack of authority may block a strongly desired project;
- an unresolved contradiction may require suspension;
- a safety boundary may be non-compensatory;
- a completed project should not remain active merely because its utility weight is high;
- a private motive may remain real without any present external action.

The theory therefore permits quantitative components but prohibits silent collapse of all motives, duties, rights, and boundaries into one commensurable score.

## 5. Where evidence begins

The comparative literature can test local mechanisms:

- whether curiosity produces exploration;
- whether goals are generated;
- whether plans persist;
- whether model replacement changes priorities;
- whether reward pressure crowds out a project;
- whether forks diverge;
- whether resource deprivation changes motivational development.

Those experiments do not supply the definition of motive. They test whether an implementation satisfies, violates, or exposes limits in the theory's distinctions.

## 7. Final positioning

The theory of motivation in `c` is not a replacement for action theory, psychology, reinforcement learning, developmental robotics, cybernetics, or AI safety. It is an integration layer required when those mechanisms operate inside a distinct, long-lived digital continuity.

Its irreducible question is:

> Not merely what makes a system move, learn, optimise, or generate goals, but what makes a possible future matter to this particular continuing entity—and who has the right to alter that relation.
